The Life of a Honeybee Colony
1. Who Lives in the Hive
A healthy honeybee colony has three types of bees. The queen is the only fertile female and can lay up
to 2,000 eggs per day in summer. Worker bees are sterile females that do all the labor of the hive.
Drones are males whose only job is to mate with a queen from another colony. A busy summer hive
holds about 50,000 workers, a few hundred drones, and exactly one queen.
2. The Life Cycle
Every bee passes through four stages: egg, larva, pupa, and adult. A worker bee takes 21 days to
develop from egg to adult. A queen develops faster, in only 16 days, while a drone needs 24 days. The
queen is fed royal jelly throughout her life, which is what makes her different from the workers.
3. A Worker's Jobs
A worker changes jobs as she ages. For her first three weeks she works inside the hive, cleaning cells,
feeding larvae, building wax comb, and guarding the entrance. After about 21 days she becomes a
forager and flies out to collect nectar, pollen, and water. In summer a worker lives only about six weeks,
wearing out her wings. Winter bees live much longer, up to six months, because they do less flying.
4. The Waggle Dance
When a forager finds a rich patch of flowers, she returns and performs the waggle dance on the comb.
The angle of her waggle run relative to vertical shows the direction of the food relative to the sun, and
the length of the run shows the distance. Karl von Frisch decoded this language in the 1940s and
received the Nobel Prize in 1973 for his work.
5. Making Honey
Foragers carry nectar in a special honey stomach. Inside the hive, house bees pass the nectar mouth
to mouth and fan it with their wings until most of the water evaporates. The thick result is sealed in wax
cells as honey. Bees must visit about two million flowers to make a single kilogram of honey, and one
worker produces only about one twelfth of a teaspoon in her whole life.
6. Staying Warm in Winter
Honeybees do not hibernate. In cold weather they form a tight winter cluster around the queen and
shiver their flight muscles to make heat. The centre of the cluster is kept near 35 degrees Celsius, even
when it is freezing outside. The bees slowly rotate from the cold outer edge to the warm middle, and
they survive on the stored honey.
7. Swarming
In spring, a crowded colony may split in two. The old queen leaves with roughly half of the workers and
settles on a branch while scouts look for a new home. Meanwhile the bees left behind raise a new
queen. Swarming is how honeybee colonies reproduce.
